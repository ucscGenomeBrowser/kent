The basics

How do I tell whether a region is regulatory?

This requires combining data from several different assays since each only measures one property often associated with regulatory regions. But there are limits what these assays can tell you about a given region.

  • Open chromatin: DNase-seq and ATAC-seq measure whether a segment of DNA is accessible to DNA-binding proteins. High signal means that region was more frequently measured as accessible. Tracks with data from both assays can be found in ENCODE4 Regulation, organized by tissue, on hg38 and mm10, and the older ENCODE3 DNase HS clusters are on hg38.
  • Histone modifications: Different histone marks are associated with different gene expression states. You often find H3K4me3 at active promoters and H3K4me1 at enhancers, while H3K27ac will be found at both active promoters and enhancers. On the other hand, H3K27me3 is found across a gene loci that is being repressed by Polycomb. The Layered H3K27Ac track overlays H3K27ac signal from several cell lines in one display, on hg19 and hg38. The cCRE tracks combine DNase, H3K4me3, H3K27ac and CTCF signal into a single classification of each element.
  • DNA methylation: Methylation in promoter regions is generally associated with silencing of the nearby gene. The CpG Islands track, which annotates regions of higher CG percent than elsewehere in the genome, overlap many promoters and are unmethylated in most tissues. This track is available for nearly every assembly, including hg19, hg38, mm10 and mm39. The DNA Methylation collection holds measurements from several sources, with hypo- and hypermethylated regions called in a range of cell types, on hg19 and hg38.
  • Transcription factor binding: There are over a thousand known human transcription factors. The binding motif of one transcription factor may be nearly identical to another, so a motif match on its own is often not enough to confirm binding at a site (see measured and predicted binding sites and the binding site tracks).
  • Conservation and constraint: phyloP and phastCons score conservation across species on hg19 and hg38; Unusually Conserved marks regions more conserved than expected, such as sequence identical between human and mouse, on hg38; and the Constraint scores collection measures how little a region varies between human genomes, on hg19 and hg38.
  • Physical contact: Enhancers can sit up to a megabase from the genes they regulate. Hi-C and Micro-C, on hg38, shows which regions are in contact in the folded genome, while CTCF sites often mark the anchors of the loops (see also finding the factors that regulate a gene).
What is the difference between measured and predicted binding sites?

A measured site comes from an experiment, where the binding activity of one transcription factor was measured in a single cell type or cell line. For example, that means that each ChIP-seq track displays only the binding activity of a single factor in a single cell type.

A predicted site comes from scanning the genome with a sequence motif. These predicted binding sites are not cell-type specific. These sequence motifs are relatively short, meaning one might match hundreds of thousands of positions in the human genome. This means that most predicted sites are not bound in vivo.

In short, a predicted track can tell you if a region could be bound, while a measured track can tell you where a factor was found in a specific cell type.

Transcription factor binding sites

How do I find transcription factor binding sites?

We have several tracks in the Regulation group that annotate transcription factor binding sites.

  • The ReMap track integrates ChIP-seq experiments for transcriptional regulators from several sources (GEO, ArrayExpress and ENCODE). It has three subtracks that offer different views of the data: all peaks per experiment; a non-redundant set in which similar targets are merged; and cis-regulatory modules. It's available on hg19, hg38, mm10, mm39 and dm6.
  • TF ChIP holds the ENCODE3 transcription factor ChIP-seq peaks, all processed with one pipeline, which makes the experiments comparable with each other: 340 factors in 129 cell types on hg38, and 338 factors in 130 cell types on hg19, where the track is named ENCODE 3 TFBS. An older clustered version of the same data, Txn Factor ChIP, is on hg19.
  • JASPAR Transcription Factors holds sites predicted by scanning the genome with the JASPAR CORE profiles, scored from 0 to 1000 by how well the sequence matches the profile. Sites scoring below 400 are hidden by default, but the cutoff can be changed on the track settings page; clicking a site opens the motif display with the sequence logo and matrix. Several JASPAR releases are included as subtracks. JASPAR 2026 is on hg38, mm39, danRer11, galGal6, dm6, ce11, ci3 and sacCer3, and JASPAR 2024 is the latest on hg19 and mm10.
The JASPAR track shows my factor binding all over my gene. Why?

Motifs are short and tolerate mismatches, meaning a motif can be found hundreds of thousands of times across the human genome, including several times in a single gene.

I cannot find my transcription factor in any track. Where else can I look?

ReMap includes the ChIP-seq experiments that were public when it was built, so a factor missing from ReMap may have no public ChIP-seq in that organism. Search the ReMap website to see if they have data not yet available in the Genome Browser. There are several other public data repositories that may have the data you're looking for:

  • The ENCODE portal, which has buttons to load the data directly to the Genome Browser (see the next question).
  • GEO, where many submissions include processed peak files, usually BED or narrowPeak, that can be loaded as a custom track on the Add Custom Tracks page. Be sure to select the right assembly when loading the data.
  • SRA holds raw reads, but these cannot be displayed in the Genome Browser until you've processed and analyzed them. This should be your last resort as it will be the most time consuming and demanding option.
How do I display ENCODE data that is not already a track?

ENCODE data can be loaded directly from the ENCODE portal, without needing to downloading any files:

  1. Search the ENCODE portal and narrow the results with the filters on the left, mainly Assay title, Target of assay (the factor), Biosample (the cell type or tissue) and Genome assembly.
  2. Click Visualize above the result list.
  3. Choose the assembly and click UCSC.

The Browser opens with every experiment in the filtered result set loaded as a track hub, so a broad search can load a very large number of tracks at once.

Each ENCODE experiment also has its own hub, at a URL of this form (with the accession of the experiment in place of ENCSR000AKO):

https://www.encodeproject.org/experiments/ENCSR000AKO/@@hub/hub.txt

which can be loaded from the My Hubs tab of the Track Hubs page, or appended to a browser URL as hgTracks?db=hg38&hubUrl= followed by the hub address.

Promoters, enhancers and other elements

How do I find the promoter of a gene?

It depends on what you mean by a promoter. It's usually the region just upstream of the transcription start site, but people use anything from 100 bp to 2 kb, and the tracks below don't all define it the same way.

If you need a well-defined promoter that you can cite, the EPDnew Promoters track (hg19, hg38, mm10) has experimentally defined promoters, each with a single precisely mapped transcription start site; on hg38 there's also a separate set for non-coding RNA promoters. The FANTOM5 track has transcription start sites found with CAGE, with expression values for each of them in a large number of tissues and cell types. It's on hg19, hg38 and mm10, and on a few other genomes including chicken, dog, rat and rhesus. The ENCODE cCRE tracks also have promoter-like elements, called from chromatin data, which cover more of the genome than EPDnew although their boundaries are less precise.

If all you need is a fixed amount of sequence upstream of a gene, the Table Browser can get that for any gene track, and our download server has files with the 1000, 2000 and 5000 bp upstream of RefSeq genes (see Obtaining promoter sequence).

How do I find enhancers and other regulatory elements?

Most people asking about enhancers are looking for GeneHancer, on hg19 and hg38, which has regulatory elements and links each one to the genes it's predicted to regulate, drawn as arcs (see the next question). VISTA Enhancers is much smaller, but every element in it was tested individually in transgenic mice, and the track lists the tissues where each one drove expression (hg19, hg38, mm10, mm39). NCBI's curated set of experimentally characterized non-coding elements is in RefSeq Functional Elements on hg38 and mm10. On hg38 there is also the MPRAs track, with results from massively parallel reporter assays: MPRA Base has 40,938 tested elements, and MPRAVarDB has tested variants (see variant effects below).

The DNase, ATAC-seq, histone and CTCF data behind many of these elements are in ENCODE4 Regulation, organized by tissue, on hg38 and mm10. That track replaced ENCODE3 Regulation as the default in July 2026, but ENCODE3 Regulation is still available on hg19 and hg38.

What do the GeneHancer arcs between elements and genes mean?

In the GeneHancer track, the arcs connect an element (the high end of the arc) to a gene (the low end). The connections come from several different sources, including eQTLs, promoter capture Hi-C, correlation of enhancer RNA and gene expression, correlation between transcription factors and possible targets, and the distance to the gene. Links that are mostly based on distance are basically the same as picking the nearest gene, so they aren't strong evidence that the element regulates that gene. There is also a double elite subset of the track that only has the elements and links with support from more than one source, which is much smaller than the full set.

What are cCREs, and which cCRE track should I use?

ENCODE's Registry of candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) is a set of regions that look like promoters, enhancers or CTCF-bound sites, based on DNase, H3K4me3, H3K27ac and CTCF data from many biosamples. As "candidates", most of them haven't been tested in an experiment. On hg38, for example, the TERT promoter is covered by the promoter-like cCRE EH38E3622530.

The ENCODE cCREs track on hg38 and mm10 has three versions of the registry. The default since July 2026 is ENCODE4 cCREs, with 2.3 million elements in human and 927,000 in mouse, and it's the version most people will want. The ENCODE4 Core Collection is a smaller subset with higher confidence, from the 170 human and 18 mouse biosamples with data for all four core assays. We also kept the older ENCODE3 cCREs, since many published papers used it. The track also has subtracks for each biosample if you want to look at one cell type.

Reading the data

What do the scores and colors in these tracks mean?

Many of these tracks follow the BED score convention, where each item has a score from 0 to 1000 and is drawn in a shade of grey, with darker items having higher scores. In most of them the score reflects how strong the signal was in the experiment the data came from. A few tracks use color in other ways, though. The JASPAR track is shaded by how well each site matches the motif, while ReMap gives every transcription factor its own color and GeneHancer uses color to show both the element type (promoter or enhancer) and its confidence level (high, medium or low). The cCRE tracks are colored by element class. What the scores and colors mean for a particular track is described on its description page, which you can get to by clicking on the track name.

The signal is higher in one region than another. Can I compare them?

That depends on the tracks and how they are scaled. In the layered tracks, such as the ENCODE4 histone and accessibility tracks on hg38, the cell types are drawn on the same vertical axis, so you can compare the different colored signals in a single view. These tracks are set to Auto-scale to data view by default, however, so the vertical range is recalculated each time you move or zoom and the same peak can be drawn at a different height somewhere else. To compare two regions, go to the track settings page, change Data view scaling to use the vertical viewing range, and set a range that works for your data. The older ENCODE3 layered tracks on hg19 and hg38 already use a fixed range.

Values from two different tracks usually can't be compared directly, since the data were produced by different labs with different pipelines and are often in different units. If you need the actual values for your regions, you can get them from the Table Browser or the Data Integrator, and the units for each track are given on its description page.

Nothing is annotated in my region. Does that mean it is not regulatory?

Not necessarily. It could mean that's not regulatory, but it could also just mean that these particular experiments did not report anything in that region.

Working with the data

How do I show data for only one cell type or tissue?

Most large regulatory tracks are collections of subtracks, often one per cell type or experiment, and many show only a summary by default. Clicking the track name opens its settings page, where individual subtracks can be selected; some tracks also have filters, such as the transcription factor filter in ReMap, which shows one factor across all of its experiments. Collections with hundreds or thousands of experiments, such as the per-experiment tracks of ENCODE4 Regulation on hg38, have a settings page with facets on the left (tissue, assay, biosample) and a table of matching experiments on the right. Track Search searches track names and descriptions across an assembly.

There is no data for my tissue. What can I do?

First, you should check if the experiment exists but is not available in the Genome Browser. For example, the ENCODE portal includes many biosamples that were not imported as native Genome Browser tracks, and which provides an easy way to visualize data in the Genome Browser (see the displaying ENCODE data section above). You can also search the public hubs list, as some may contain more tissue-specific regulatory data than what's available natively in the Browser.

Beyond that, you might consider closely related cell types, or even annotations not specific to one cell type. For example, the cCREs tracks pool many biosamples into a single classification, while conservation does not depend on experimental data. But, these run the risk of not being specific enough to describe what's happening in your cell type of interest.

I have a gene. How do I find the factors that regulate it?

You will need to go to your gene locus and turn on several tracks to find regulatory elements. A general workflow might look like this:

  1. First, navigate to the gene, e.g. BRCA1 at chr17:43,044,295-43,125,483 on hg38, and zoom out to include some of the non-coding sequence around it.
  2. Next, turn on the GeneHancer track, which shows distant enhancers for a gene.
  3. After that, turn on the ReMap or TF ChIP tracks. You can use these to identify the transcription factors with binding activity within GeneHancer elements. Note: check which cell types show peaks so that you're looking at only those relevant to your question.
  4. If no relevant cell type has been assayed, turn on the JASPAR track. Look for predicted binding motifs within the GeneHancer elements, which you can use as candidates for experimental testing.

For many genes or regions at once, the Data Integrator intersects two or more tracks and returns a table, and the Table Browser does the same for a region or a list of genes.

I have a variant. How can I tell if it affects regulation?

MPRAVarDB, in the MPRAs collection on hg38, holds 239,028 variants tested for allelic effect in reporter assays, from 18 MPRA studies in more than 30 cell lines. These variants were identified using GWAS and eQTL fine-mapping as well as saturation mutagenesis of 20 disease-associated elements. This means that they are concentrated at loci already associated with a disease or trait. For example, in the TERT promoter (chr5:1,294,989-1,295,246 in hg38) there are 3,896 entries across 259 consecutive positions, 1,464 of them with an FDR below 0.05. But this kind of density is not evenly distributed across the genome.

For variants not in MPRAVarDB, three tracks in Phenotype and Disease Associations give a predicted score for every possible single-base substitution; AlphaGenome and PromoterAI are in the Deleteriousness Predictions collection on hg38.

  • CADD combines conservation and regulatory signal to give one score per substitution. Thus, you could use it, in part, to understand how a variant might affect a regulatory region. Scores are PHRED scaled (meaning 10 marks the top 10% of substitutions genome-wide, 20 the top 1%, and 30 the top 0.1%). Version 1.7 is on hg19 and hg38.
  • AlphaGenome, from Google DeepMind, provides a "Variant Impact" score that combines predicted effects on expression, splicing, chromatin accessibility and transcription factor binding. There is one track per alternate allele (i.e. A/T/C/G), which uses the same PHRED scale as CADD. On hg38.
  • PromoterAI, from Illumina, scores substitutions in proximal promoters only, on hg38.
What is available for assemblies other than human and mouse?

The regulatory data for non-human assemblies is limited.

For mouse, the mm10 assembly has the most regulatory tracks, though mm39 is catching up and now includes JASPAR, ReMap and VISTA. You can use "QuickLift" (under "View > In Other Genomes (Convert)") to convert any regulatory tracks you have enabled from one assembly to another, such as from mm10 to mm39.

JASPAR is available for most non-human and non-mouse organisms, including zebrafish, fly, worm, chicken, sea squirt and yeast. This is primarily because JASPAR is based on searching for sequence motifs, which only requires the genome sequence itself. ReMap, which pulls in data from several repositories, is also available for fly.

Check our public hubs list to see if there are data available from external groups for your assembly of interest.

Limits of the evidence

If a region has all of this evidence, is it definitely regulatory?

Not necessarily. These tracks record biochemical activity (e.g., binding, accessibility, histone marks). For example, early ENCODE results demonstrated activity over roughly 80% of the genome, which raised debates over what level of activity was needed to label a region as "functional" (Graur et al. 2013; Kellis et al. 2014). Experimental tests of predicted regulatory regions give mixed results:

  • None of ten single limb enhancer deletions in mouse changed limb morphology, while deleting pairs near the same gene did (Osterwalder et al. 2018).
  • After knockdown of 59 transcription factors in one cell line, only a small subset of the genes bound by each factor changed expression (Cusanovich et al. 2014).
  • In fly, five of the six most heavily bound regions drove patterned expression, against one of nine lightly bound regions (Fisher et al. 2012).
  • In mouse retina, Crx-bound sequences drove expression and unbound sequences with the same number of Crx motifs did not (White et al. 2013).
  • Of more than 2,000 ENCODE chromatin state segments tested in K562 cells, those labeled Weak Enhancer drove more expression than those labeled Enhancer; these come from an earlier segmentation, not the cCRE registry (Kwasnieski et al. 2014).

Beyond that, some functional binding sites are not conserved while others don't match any JASPAR profiles closely enough to appear in the track.
